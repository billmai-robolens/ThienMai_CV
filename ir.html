Four robot arms that cook dinner, and stop for people

Industrial Robotics (41013), team of three. The brief asked for a pick-and-place robot system for the home or workplace with safety built in. We built a robot kitchen in the Swift simulator where four arms fetch food from a fridge, chop it, cook it and plate it. I wrote the motion code for all four robots, modelled the CAD meshes and put the whole environment together.

Section01

The brief: a helpful robot that is safe to stand next to

SafeCo, the fictional client, wanted to sell small robot systems for homes and workplaces. Every group had to use one arm from the Robotics Toolbox plus one new 6-DOF-or-more industrial arm per student, all working in one shared workspace.

The system needed a teach GUI with joint and Cartesian jogging, an e-stop in both hardware and software where resuming takes two separate actions, safety equipment in the scene, a reaction to a simulated sensor, and active avoidance of a collision we forced into the robot’s path.

Section03

My arm: a Mitsubishi RV-5AS on a 1.7 m rail

My new robot was a Mitsubishi RV-5AS six-axis arm mounted on a linear rail, which makes it a 7-DOF robot that can reach all three fridge shelves. I wrote its standard DH model, a prismatic first joint with 1.7 m of travel and then the six revolute joints, and modelled a CAD mesh for every link.

Each mesh is aligned to its DH frame through a test pose and per-link transforms in a scaled 3D robot class, so the rendered arm moves exactly with the kinematic model. I also rebuilt the UR3’s rail to a fixed length to fit the kitchen.

Section04

Motion: IK for poses, RMRC for straight lines

Inverse kinematics finds the joint configuration at each cabinet handle and each pick and drop pose. Moves between free-space waypoints use joint-space trajectories (jtraj). Opening and closing a cabinet door needs the hand to follow a straight line, so those moves use resolved-rate motion control (RMRC), with separate solvers for the 6-DOF and 7-DOF arms.

One shared move function drives every robot, so the e-stop, the light curtain and collision checks apply to all four arms at each step.

Section05

Safety: four ways to stop, one way to resume

Hardware e-stop: an Arduino button sends a stop over serial and every arm freezes immediately. Releasing it only requests a resume, which must then be confirmed in the GUI: two separate actions, as the brief required.

Light curtain: a simulated person walks towards the kitchen entrance. When their body crosses the curtain zone, the robots stop.

Collision avoidance: an obstacle can be dropped into a robot’s path. The planner checks every link against it with line–triangle intersection tests along a path interpolated at 1° steps. If a collision is predicted, it searches for collision-free joint states above the floor and replans around the obstacle instead of just stopping.

Vision: a RealSense camera watches for red objects, standing in for fire, and triggers the e-stop.

Section06

Teach GUI

The GUI jogs any of the four robots joint by joint with sliders, or in Cartesian space (x, y, z and roll, pitch, yaw), checks joint limits before each step, and carries the software e-stop and resume buttons that stay in sync with the hardware button.
